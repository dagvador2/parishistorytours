The First World War ends in nineteen eighteen. The Germans sign the armistice in a railway carriage, in the forest of Compiègne. The following year, they're forced to sign the Treaty of Versailles — a treaty that brings Germany to its knees.

The treaty hit Germany on three fronts. Territorial: Germany loses fifteen per cent of its territory and all of its colonies. Financial: one hundred and thirty-two billion gold marks in reparations — an enormous sum. And military: no more armour, no more air force, and an army capped at one hundred thousand men.

Those penalties fed a deep desire for revenge in Germany. The crash of nineteen twenty-nine sent poverty and unemployment soaring. Fascism spread. And Hitler took power in January nineteen thirty-three, exactly ten years after his failed putsch. Once in power, he starts tearing up the Treaty of Versailles clause by clause — he refuses to pay what is owed and, above all, he rebuilds the German army.

France and the United Kingdom responded with pacifism. The central figure is the British Prime Minister, Neville Chamberlain. He wanted to preserve peace at almost any price. Every time Hitler broke a clause of the treaty, the French and the British said: we're not going to war over that. Peace matters more.

That way of thinking culminated in the Munich Agreement, signed in September nineteen thirty-eight. Hitler, Chamberlain, France and Italy shake hands over the fate of a region of Czechoslovakia called the Sudetenland — a part of the country with a large German-speaking population. Hitler is given the green light to take it. Chamberlain goes home waving the agreement, proud of what he calls "peace for our time". Six months later, Hitler breaks that treaty, just as he had broken the Treaty of Versailles, and invades the rest of Czechoslovakia by force.

That was the last straw. France and the United Kingdom then decided that Poland would be the red line Hitler could not cross.
