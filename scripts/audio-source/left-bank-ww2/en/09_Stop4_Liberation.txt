Welcome to the final stop of the tour. You're facing Notre-Dame, with the Île de la Cité in front of you.

But the building this stop is really about is the one immediately to the right of Notre-Dame, on the same island: the Préfecture de Police de Paris.

The Préfecture was the first stronghold seized by the resistance during the insurrection of August nineteen forty-four. It is where the Liberation of Paris really began.

Before we get into what happened here, you need to understand the broader picture. Paris in August nineteen forty-four came terrifyingly close to suffering the same fate as Warsaw.

Warsaw's insurrection began on the first of August nineteen forty-four, just three weeks before the Paris uprising. It lasted more than sixty days. By the end, eighteen thousand Polish soldiers and one hundred and seventy thousand civilians were dead. Eighty-five percent of the city was destroyed. The Soviets, who were within striking distance, did not come to the Poles' aid.

Now look at Paris in August nineteen forty-four. A city occupied by the Nazis. An insurrection started by its own people. Allies who were not originally planning to liberate the city. And a new German commander appointed by a dictator with nothing left to lose. Sounds familiar?

Three reasons the Allies didn't plan to liberate Paris straight away. One: the fear of street-by-street fighting like Stalingrad, costly in both soldiers and civilians. Two: Paris was not strategically essential. Going through it would delay the American advance on Berlin, where the race against the Soviets had become the priority. Three: the logistical nightmare of suddenly having to feed three million starving Parisians once the city was liberated.

The Allies had landed in Normandy on the sixth of June nineteen forty-four, and the original plan was to sweep east straight for Berlin, bypassing Paris. What changed their minds was the insurrection itself, and what Raoul Nordling — the Swedish consul, more on him in a minute — was able to tell Eisenhower about Von Choltitz. By the twenty-second of August, the decision had been taken: Leclerc and his men would rush in. The French Second Armoured Division and the US Fourth Infantry Division would come to Paris's aid.

Let me introduce you to the main characters of that terrible week.

On the German side: General Dietrich von Choltitz, brought to Paris by Hitler himself on the tenth of August. His mission: keep Paris German, and crush any insurrection without mercy.

On the French side, two camps were competing for political power for after the liberation. The Communists, who led most of the resistance fighters inside Paris, under Colonel Henri Rol-Tanguy. And the Gaullists, loyal to General Charles de Gaulle. They controlled most of the police force inside Paris, and crucially they had General Philippe Leclerc — who was leading De Gaulle's French Second Armoured Division, cruising eastward alongside the Allies after the Normandy landings.

By mid-August, Paris was boiling. The police, the gas workers, the electricity workers, the transport workers — all on strike. The Germans were openly preparing to defend the city. Von Choltitz had told the mayor of Paris that he was ready to destroy every bridge in the city and every power station.

The Gaullists wanted to delay the insurrection — it would be a bloodbath for Parisians. The Communists wanted to launch it immediately, to take political control of the city by force of arms. The Communists got their way. The insurrection began on the nineteenth of August nineteen forty-four — the one thousand five hundred and eighteenth day of the German occupation.

Even though the Communists had wanted to fire the first shot, the Gaullists heard about it and moved fast. It would have been too dangerous to let the Communists alone claim the symbol of the insurrection. So a senior policeman, Yves Bayet, led the Paris police force in seizing the Préfecture de Police for General de Gaulle. He then walked to the terrace of the café Les Deux Magots and told Charles Luizet — De Gaulle's trusted lieutenant, parachuted into France just seven days earlier — that the Préfecture was his.

It was the first time the sound of gunfire had echoed through Paris since the Commune of eighteen seventy-one. At three-thirty in the afternoon, the Germans sent tanks. Two Panthers and a captured Renault rolled onto the parvis of Notre-Dame — directly in front of you, where the tourists now stand — and opened fire. The main door of the Préfecture exploded.

Faced with this open revolt, Von Choltitz had to act fast. His plan: a Luftwaffe air strike on the Préfecture at four in the morning on the twentieth of August, followed by a ground assault by the tanks. The Préfecture would be flattened.

It didn't happen. The Swedish consul-general in Paris, Raoul Nordling, intervened and proposed a ceasefire that suited both sides. Von Choltitz needed time to consolidate, the resistance needed to resupply. The truce was agreed.

It did not hold. The Communists pushed hard against it — they wanted the insurrection to keep its momentum. Rol-Tanguy, that day, said the line that would become famous: "Paris is worth two hundred thousand dead."

All over Paris, the city organised for war. Clandestine printing presses now produced flyers with Molotov cocktail recipes. Pharmacies became improvised arsenals. The flats of resistance medical students became field hospitals.

On the night of the twenty-second of August, after Nordling crossed the Allied lines and pleaded with Eisenhower and Bradley, the decision was finally taken: the French Second Armoured Division under General Leclerc, supported by the US Fourth Infantry, would rush to Paris. Leclerc had been ordered to wait. He started moving anyway.

On the twenty-third of August, Von Choltitz received a direct order from Hitler himself. If the Allies were to reach Paris, they should find only ruins. Across the city, the explosives were already in place. The forty-two bridges. The Parliament. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The Luxembourg Palace. The two hotels on Place de la Concorde. The Invalides. Everything was wired. Choltitz was waiting on the final order.

By the morning of the twenty-fourth of August, the situation for the resistance fighters inside the Préfecture was desperate. They were almost out of ammunition. Luizet and his deputy Pisani called the police station in Longjumeau, thirty-five kilometres south of Paris, to ask where the Allied divisions were. The man on the other end held the phone to the window and said: "Can you hear that? They're right here."

And then there's a story I really want to tell you.

On the afternoon of the twenty-fourth of August, hours before the Allied tanks would enter the city, General Leclerc ordered two French pilots to carry out an extraordinary mission. Their job: fly over Paris and drop a written message into the courtyard of the Préfecture de Police, to tell the resistance fighters inside: hold on, we are coming.

Captain Jean Callet was the pilot. Lieutenant Étienne Mantoux was in the back, holding the message. The mission launched around five in the afternoon. They flew low, hiding in the clouds where they could. By six they were over Paris and could see the objective ahead — but the Germans could see them too.

Eight hundred metres from the Préfecture, as Callet was following the Seine, he abruptly nosed the plane into a dive. He wanted the Germans to think he'd been hit and was crashing, to make them stop firing. At the last possible moment, he pulled up — and Mantoux, in the back, dropped the message. It landed inside the Préfecture courtyard.

Getting away, they flew as close to the rooftops as they dared, zigzagging hard. The landing gear of the plane was shot up over southern Paris. The plane had thirteen bullet holes when they finally landed in a field in Allied-held territory, tipped over on its right wing.

Jean Callet survived the war and became a general in the French army. He died in two thousand and six. Étienne Mantoux was not so lucky — he was killed on the twenty-ninth of April nineteen forty-five, less than ten days before Victory in Europe.

At nine twenty-two in the evening on the twenty-fourth of August, the first detachment of the French Second Armoured Division, led by Captain Raymond Dronne, reached the Hôtel de Ville. The resistance fighters inside burst into La Marseillaise. The song spread to the streets. On the radio, an announcer asked all the priests of Paris to ring their bells — for the first time in four years, since they had stayed silent throughout the Occupation. The great thirteen-tonne bell of Notre-Dame, the one named Emmanuel, the one directly in front of you now, rang out around nine-thirty.

On the morning of the twenty-fifth of August, the French Second Armoured Division and the US Fourth Infantry made their proper entrance into Paris. The streets erupted. Parisians threw everything they had — fruit, cheese, bottles of wine and champagne saved for exactly this moment — at the soldiers.

And this is where our tour ends — facing Notre-Dame, with the Préfecture de Police that started the insurrection just over the river, and the cathedral whose bells rang to mark the moment the Liberation became real.
