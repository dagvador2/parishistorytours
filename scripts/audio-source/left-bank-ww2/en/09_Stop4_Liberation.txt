Welcome to the last stop of the tour. You're facing Notre-Dame de Paris.

But the building this stop is really about is the one directly opposite Notre-Dame, the Préfecture de Police de Paris.

The Préfecture de Police is the first stronghold seized by the resistance during the August nineteen forty-four insurrection. That's where the liberation of Paris really began, on the morning of the nineteenth of August, and it's also here that a spectacular air mission took place on the twenty-fourth of August nineteen forty-four. But I'm going to keep you in suspense on that one for now.

In the wider theatre of the war, the German army had finally lost the upper hand. That becomes concrete with the Allied landings in Normandy in June nineteen forty-four. From then on, the objective is clear for the Allied armies: reach Berlin and end the Second World War. But between Normandy and Berlin lies Paris, and the question then arises: should Paris be liberated straight away?

Symbolically: yes. Logistically: it's a nightmare. Once liberated, the capital has to be supplied, which will take men, fuel, food and so on. That's a considerable waste of time, and unacceptable to the American generals leading the Allied advance: at first, they want to go around Paris.

But the Paris insurrection starts on the nineteenth of August, and it's about to turn all of their plans upside down. Before going into the events of the Paris insurrection in detail, let me first introduce you to the main players of that terrible week.

On the German side: General Dietrich von Choltitz, appointed to Paris by Hitler himself on the tenth of August. His mission: to make sure Paris stays in German hands, and to crush any insurrection without mercy. He has been warned: if the situation gets out of hand, his orders will be to leave nothing but a field of ruins. He has twenty thousand men in the city, and three hundred tanks.

On the French side, and this is a little more delicate, two camps are fighting over political power for the aftermath of the liberation. The communists, who led most of the resistance fighters inside Paris itself, under the command of Colonel Henri Rol-Tanguy. And on the other side the Gaullists, loyal to General Charles de Gaulle. They controlled most of the police forces in Paris, and above all they had General Philippe Leclerc — who commanded the Second Armoured Division, fighting alongside the Allies after the landings in Normandy.

The insurrection was necessary for both French camps: it would prepare the liberation of the city. But the date they each wanted for it was very different between de Gaulle and Rol-Tanguy. The Gaullists wanted to delay the insurrection — so as to wait for the Allied armies to move on the city, so that de Gaulle could easily be put in power with the help of those armies. The communists led by Rol-Tanguy, for their part, wanted to launch it immediately, to drive the Germans out of Paris without American help, which would have given them a great deal of weight. And in the end, the communists got their way. The insurrection begins far earlier than originally planned. It starts on the nineteenth of August nineteen forty-four — right in front of you. The two thousand Paris police officers barricaded themselves that morning inside the Préfecture, and began firing on every German vehicle passing nearby.

It was the first time gunfire had echoed through Paris since the Paris Commune of eighteen seventy-one. At half past three in the afternoon, the Germans send tanks right here. Two Panthers and a captured Renault roll onto the square in front of Notre-Dame — right in front of you, where the tourists are standing today — and open fire. The main door of the Préfecture is blown apart.

Faced with this open revolt, von Choltitz has to act fast. His plan: an air strike by the Luftwaffe, the German air force, on the Préfecture de Paris at four in the morning on the twentieth of August, followed by a ground assault by the tanks. The Préfecture would be completely flattened.

In the end, that didn't happen. A truce between the French and the Germans is finally signed on the evening of the nineteenth of August. Von Choltitz needed time to consolidate his positions; the resistance, for its part, needed to resupply.

That pause in the fighting would last only two days in the end. The communists pushed hard against it — they wanted the insurrection to keep its momentum. Rol-Tanguy, that day, said the line that would stay infamous: "A free Paris is well worth two hundred thousand dead." The fighting resumes on the morning of the twenty-second of August, and the barricades start going up across the capital.

On the night of the twenty-second of August, Eisenhower and Bradley, the two generals in charge of the Allied advance, finally take the decision to drive on Paris. General Leclerc's French Second Armoured Division, supported by the American Fourth Infantry Division, set course for Paris.

On the twenty-third of August, von Choltitz receives a direct order from Hitler himself. If the Allies reach Paris, they must find nothing but ruins. All across the city, the explosives were already in place. The forty-two bridges. The Parliament. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The Palais du Luxembourg. The two hotels on the Place de la Concorde. Les Invalides. Everything was rigged and ready to blow. Choltitz was waiting for the final order.

On the morning of the twenty-fourth of August, the situation for the resistance fighters inside the Préfecture was critical. They were almost out of ammunition. They were desperately calling police stations south-west of Paris, barely a few kilometres away, to find out where the Allied divisions were. The Allied armies were so close, and yet so far, for all those police officers holed up in the Préfecture.

And here there's a story I really want to tell you.

On the afternoon of the twenty-fourth of August, Leclerc realises he won't manage to send the bulk of his troops into Paris that day. German resistance in the south-west is too strong. He's afraid the Préfecture de Police might fall back into German hands. And it's a central building in Paris; it's critical that the Germans don't take it back. To make sure the police hold it to the end, General Leclerc decides to launch an air mission with one purpose: to deliver a message to the police officers holed up inside.

Captain Jean Callet was at the controls. Lieutenant Étienne Mantoux was in the back, with the message. He takes off around five in the afternoon aboard a light American reconnaissance aircraft called a Piper Cub. He approaches Paris, and the plan is to follow the Seine to get close to the Préfecture, playing with the clouds to stay hidden as long as possible. As he nears the building, the pilot dives right over it and Étienne Mantoux, behind him, throws out the message tied to a stone. He aims true, and the message lands in the courtyard of the Préfecture.

They then carry on along the Seine. The aircraft is badly hit over south-east Paris, but they manage to get back to their base in Normandy, where they had taken off two hours earlier. Despite thirteen bullet holes in the airframe, and a burst left tyre on the landing gear, they eventually touch down safe and sound. The police officers in the Préfecture, having received a message from General Leclerc himself, would manage to hold the building until the Allies arrived the next day.

Finally, on the morning of the twenty-fifth of August, the French Second Armoured Division and the American Fourth Infantry Division make their official entry into Paris. Fighting rages all across the city. But around three in the afternoon, von Choltitz, the commander of the German armed forces, is taken prisoner. He is brought to Montparnasse station where he signs the German surrender with Leclerc. And then, one by one, the German strongholds give in, and German soldiers are escorted through the streets towards prisoner camps. By the evening of the twenty-fifth of August, Paris is liberated, and the fighting has stopped for good.

And this is where our tour ends — beside Notre-Dame, with the Préfecture de Police opposite, where the insurrection was set off. On the twenty-sixth of August, de Gaulle would claim victory in Paris by marching down the Champs-Élysées in front of more than two million Parisians. The war in Paris was over. There we are.
