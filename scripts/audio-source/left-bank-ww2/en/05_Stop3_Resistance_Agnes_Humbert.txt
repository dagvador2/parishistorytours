Welcome to the third stop. You're at the corner of Rue Monsieur-le-Prince and Rue de Vaugirard, in the sixth arrondissement.

Nothing here marks the spot — and that is partly the point.

In the autumn of nineteen forty, a small group of Paris intellectuals began meeting in this neighbourhood to plan what would become France's first organised resistance network. These streets, this quarter — they were the kind of unremarkable Left Bank backstreets where a resistance cell could meet without drawing attention.

I'm going to tell you the story of one of those meetings, and of the woman at its centre. Her name was Agnès Humbert. She was an art historian, a museum curator, and she became one of the founders of the first major resistance network in Paris.

Chronologically, at the previous stop we left things on the fourteenth of June nineteen forty — the day Paris fell. Eight days later, on the twenty-second of June, France signed the armistice. After that, slowly, the Parisians who had fled south started coming home. So did the staff of the great Paris museums, including Agnès Humbert, an art historian working at the Musée National des Arts et Traditions Populaires.

She returned at the beginning of August nineteen forty. Very quickly, she noticed what had changed. Everything was now labelled in German on top of French. Museums were free of charge for German soldiers. And entire exhibitions had been altered or removed. Photographs of the USSR that Agnès Humbert had taken were gone. So were photos of the nineteen thirty-six French general strikes. The Occupation reached into every institution.

Before we get to the resistance network itself, a word about why museums sit at the heart of this story. Every great Paris museum was caught up in the war. The Jeu de Paume, just over the river, became the central depot for art looted from Jewish collections across Europe. Hermann Goering — the second man of the Reich — visited the Jeu de Paume at least twenty times, hand-picking the pieces he wanted shipped to Germany.

Across town, at the Trocadéro, the Musée de l'Homme — an anthropology museum opened just three years earlier — became something stranger: a meeting point for intellectuals who refused to accept the Occupation. The network we're about to talk about was named after it.

Agnès Humbert wrote in her diary that summer: "It's as if we were rats in a trap." She had decided she could not live with the Occupation. The question was — what to do about it?

When you decide to resist, the first thing you do is look for allies. That's what she did. Her closest ally was Jean Cassou, a friend and fellow museum curator. Together they began meeting trusted acquaintances, one by one.

By early September nineteen forty, they had gathered a small group of around ten people they trusted absolutely. They met every week, often at fourteen Rue de l'Abbaye, in the sixth arrondissement — a few streets from where you're standing right now. At their meeting place, even the small detail of the clock mattered: it was kept on French time, not on the German time the Occupation had imposed across France. A tiny, daily act of defiance.

Their goal was to inform. They wanted to publish a clandestine newspaper — to tell Parisians the truths the censored press would not. They started small: hand-written notes on scraps of paper, left on metro benches, in mailboxes, on café tables. By the end of September they had a roneo, an early stencil duplicator, and could produce dozens of copies at once.

On the fifteenth of December nineteen forty, the group published the first edition of their clandestine newspaper. They called it Résistance — and the word would stick. After the war, this is what we would call the whole movement.

They wrote about the military situation as they understood it; messages of hope and motivation; and on the practical side, detailed accounts of how French food was being shipped to Germany — precise counts of cows, pigs, chickens, and wine taken from French farms. The point was to make Parisians see what occupation actually was, beneath the polite surface.

The newspaper was meant to come out every two weeks. They managed just four editions before the network was dismantled.

By early nineteen forty-one the Gestapo had arrived in Paris and was focusing on networks like this one. The problem with the Musée de l'Homme network was that it was growing fast — absorbing smaller networks, diversifying its work. At one point Agnès Humbert hid a Polish pilot. The network was also brought plans of a German submarine base on the Atlantic coast by a French officer.

And eventually — as is almost always the case with networks that grow too quickly — they brought in someone they should not have trusted. His name was Albert Gaveau. He worked for the Gestapo. One by one, people began to be arrested. Agnès Humbert was arrested on the thirteenth of April nineteen forty-one.

The Germans came for her at the hospital where she was visiting her mother. They drove her home to her flat and searched it. She was interrogated at the Gestapo headquarters on Rue des Saussaies, then moved through a series of Paris prisons. The network was destroyed. She survived the camps, returned to France after the war, published her diary as Notre Guerre in nineteen forty-six, and lived quietly until her death in nineteen sixty-three.
