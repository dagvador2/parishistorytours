In the autumn of nineteen forty, a small group of Parisian intellectuals began meeting in this neighbourhood to plan what would become the first organised resistance network in Paris. These streets, this quarter — this was exactly the kind of unremarkable little Left Bank street where a resistance cell could meet without drawing attention.

I'm going to tell you the story of one of those meetings, and of the woman at the centre of it. Her name was Agnès Humbert. She was an art historian, a museum curator, and she became one of the founders of the first major resistance network in Paris.

Chronologically, at the previous stop we left off on the fourteenth of June nineteen forty — the day Paris fell. Eight days later, on the twenty-second of June, France signs the armistice, and the country is split in two. After that, slowly, the Parisians who had fled south begin to come home. So do the staff of the great Paris museums, among them Agnès Humbert, an art historian at the Musée de l'Homme, let me remind you. If you remember the photograph of Hitler in front of the Eiffel Tower at the previous stop, that museum sits on the right-hand side of the square as you look at the Tower.

Agnès Humbert returns to Paris in early August nineteen forty. Very quickly, she notices what has changed. Everything is now labelled in German above the French. Museums are free for German soldiers. And whole exhibitions have been altered or removed. For instance, the photographs of the USSR, the old Russia, that Agnès Humbert had taken have vanished from her museum. The Occupation has worked its way into every institution.

Agnès Humbert writes in her diary that summer: "It is as if we were rats in a trap." She decided she could not live with the Occupation. The question was — what do we do?

And when you decide to resist, the first thing you do is look for allies. And that's what she did. Her closest ally was Jean Cassou, a friend and fellow curator. Together, they began meeting people they could trust, one by one.

In early September nineteen forty, they had gathered a small group of about ten people they trusted absolutely. They met every week on Rue Monsieur-le-Prince, the street you're standing on right now, and in their meeting place even the smallest detail mattered: the clock stayed on French time, not on the German time the Occupation had imposed across France. A tiny act of defiance, every single day.

Their aim was to inform. They wanted to publish an underground newspaper — to tell Parisians the truths the German-censored press would not print. They started small: simple words written by hand on scraps of paper, left on métro seats, in letterboxes, on café tables, anonymously. But they get more organised as they go, finding paper, ink, printing machines.

And finally, on the fifteenth of December nineteen forty, the group publishes the first issue of its underground newspaper. They call it Résistance — the title says it all.

That paper, barely four small pages long, carries no images at all. It's a goldmine of what was called at the time counter-propaganda. They wrote about the military situation as they understood it; messages of hope and encouragement; and on the practical side, detailed accounts of French food being shipped to Germany — the exact numbers of cows, pigs and chickens taken from French farms. The idea was to show Parisians what the Occupation really was, in order to push readers to join the Resistance movement.

That newspaper, the one you can see, was meant to come out every two weeks. They would only manage four issues before the network was dismantled.

In early nineteen forty-one, the Gestapo arrived in Paris and was focusing on networks like this one, which was the earliest form of resistance. The problem with the Musée de l'Homme network was that it was growing fast — it was absorbing smaller networks and diversifying its activity as it went.

Obviously, the more people there are inside a network, the more you can undertake. But the greater the risk of letting in someone working for the Germans. And in February nineteen forty-one, they brought Albert Gaveau into the network. He was also working for the Gestapo. One by one, the members began to be arrested, or fled to the southern zone. Agnès Humbert herself is arrested on the thirteenth of April nineteen forty-one.

The Germans came for her at the hospital where she was visiting her mother. They took her back to her home and searched her flat. She is then interrogated at Gestapo headquarters, on Rue des Saussaies in the eighth arrondissement, then moved through several Paris prisons. And finally, in January nineteen forty-two, the trial of the sixteen people arrested in this network takes place. Of the sixteen, ten will be sentenced to death, and the other six sentenced to forced labour in Germany. These would be the worst years of Agnès Humbert's life.

But she survived the camp. She came home to France after the war. She published her diary, titled Notre Guerre — Our War — in nineteen forty-six, and then lived quietly until her death in nineteen sixty-three.

I do recommend her book, which is in fact a personal diary. And as you can imagine, keeping a diary was particularly dangerous under the Occupation. So there aren't many works of that kind.
