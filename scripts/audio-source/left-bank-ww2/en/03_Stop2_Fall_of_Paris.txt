Welcome to the second stop. You're now standing in front of the Palais du Luxembourg, home of the French Senate since seventeen ninety-nine. In nineteen forty, it was the seat of the upper house of the French Parliament.

It was here, on the twenty-first of May nineteen forty, that Prime Minister Paul Reynaud gave one of the most important speeches of the entire war — the speech that told the French people that the situation was catastrophic.

We'll come back to that speech in a few minutes. First, let's set the scene.

At the last stop, we left things on the third of September nineteen thirty-nine, the day France and the United Kingdom declared war on Germany. Everything changed in Paris that day. Theatres, cinemas, and music halls all closed. Sandbags went up around monuments. Gas masks were distributed.

But then, on the front, almost nothing happened. Poland was crushed in a month, with Soviet help. But on the Western front, the Germans did not attack. Life in Paris slowly returned to normal — cinemas and theatres reopened, restaurants filled again. The French called this period the drôle de guerre, the phoney war. It lasted eight months.

On the twenty-sixth of February nineteen forty, the first German reconnaissance plane flew directly over Paris. That was a turning point. The new Prime Minister Paul Reynaud, who had taken office a month earlier, knew that war was inevitable — and he started preparing Parisians for it.

From the first of April nineteen forty, food restrictions began. Special breads were banned. Butter disappeared from restaurant tables. Bakeries and butcher shops opened on alternating days of the week. None of this was dramatic yet — but it was a clear signal that the easy months were over.

And on the tenth of May nineteen forty, the easy months ended for good.

The German offensive came in two phases. Phase one: the Germans attacked in Belgium and the Netherlands — exactly as the French commander-in-chief, General Maurice Gamelin, had predicted. He moved one million men north to meet them. Phase two: on the thirteenth of May, the Germans attacked lower — through the Ardennes forest, which the French generals had declared impassable by armoured columns. Using a combined force of tanks and Stuka dive-bombers, they crossed the Ardennes and the Meuse river in two days, slicing through the thin French defensive line.

And just like that, the road to Paris was open.

On the fifteenth of May at six in the morning, the Minister of Defence calls Paul Reynaud — he is the only person in the government who really understands what has just happened. His words: "Everything is lost. The road to Paris is open. There is nothing left to stop the Germans from reaching the capital."

Reynaud spends the fifteenth of May assessing the damage. On the sixteenth at nine in the morning, he convenes an emergency war committee at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs on the Quai d'Orsay, just across the river from where we are now.

Three urgent questions dominate the meeting. One: evacuate the government? Two: evacuate the population? Three: what to do with all the classified documents in Paris? The meeting was chaotic. The Ministry of Transport admitted it had neither the trucks nor the trains to move the government within two days. So it was decided that the government would stay until the very last moment, and the few trucks available would be used to move the archives.

But as the meeting was coming to a close, people in the room began to hear muffled noises from the courtyard outside. The employees of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs were dumping every classified file out of the windows into the courtyard. Once the pile was high enough, they poured petrol on it and set it on fire. The order to burn the files had come from the Secretary General of the Ministry, Alexis Léger, who knew there was no way to evacuate a hundred tonnes of archives in time.

On the night of the sixteenth of May, the news came in: the Germans were not heading for Paris. They were heading for the coast, to trap the Allied armies in the north.

A crucial point: while all this was unfolding inside the government, the people of Paris had absolutely no idea how bad things were. Censorship saw to that. The newspaper headlines on the sixteenth of May told Parisians that the Germans had been halted, and that strong counter-attacks were underway. None of it was true.

Now, back to the palace in front of you. On the twenty-first of May nineteen forty — five days after the great fear — Paul Reynaud came here to address the Senate. This was the speech where, for the first time, the political class was told the truth. He didn't hide it. He spoke of a rout of the French army, of a fault, perhaps a crime, in the conduct of operations. He announced that General Maxime Weygand would replace Gamelin as commander-in-chief, and that Marshal Philippe Pétain — the hero of Verdun — would join the government as deputy Prime Minister. Two years later, Pétain would be the man leading Vichy and the collaboration with the Germans.

Meanwhile, the cooperation between the French and the British armies began to fall apart. The British were realising that France might fall, and that they needed to bring their two hundred and fifty thousand men home to defend their own island. The result was Dunkirk: between the twenty-sixth of May and the third of June, the entire British Expeditionary Force, along with a portion of French soldiers, was evacuated across the Channel.

On the third of June, Paris was bombed. Many people think Paris was never bombed in the Second World War, because it was declared an open city. That's true — but only from the twelfth of June. On the third of June nineteen forty, German bombers struck factories in the fifteenth arrondissement. More than two hundred and fifty people died.

On the tenth of June, Italy declared war on France. The German armies were now at the gates of the city. By the evening of the tenth, every minister had fled Paris. And so had the Parisians. No clear instructions had been given to the population — but the exodus was massive. Of the two point eight million Parisians, only around seven hundred thousand stayed. About seventy-five percent of the population had fled by the fourteenth of June.

Paris was declared an open city on the twelfth of June. The Germans entered the capital early in the morning of the fourteenth of June. The Nazi flag went up everywhere — on the Élysée Palace, on the Arc de Triomphe, on the Eiffel Tower. German military headquarters were set up in the Crillon hotel, on Place de la Concorde. On the morning of the fifteenth of June, every German unit paraded down the Champs-Élysées to show the world that Paris had fallen.

A week later, Hitler made his only ever visit to Paris. He arrived around six in the morning on the twenty-third of June, toured the city — the Opéra, the Trocadéro, Napoleon's tomb at the Invalides — and was gone by nine. Three hours in the city he had dreamed of conquering his whole adult life.

And finally, on the twenty-second of June nineteen forty, France signed the armistice. Hitler had it signed in the very same railway carriage in the Compiègne forest where the Germans had signed in nineteen eighteen. The carriage was then taken to Berlin as a trophy.

That's the fall of Paris in thirty-five days. From here, we head towards the resistance.
