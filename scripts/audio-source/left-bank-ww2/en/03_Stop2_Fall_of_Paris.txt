It was here, on the twenty-first of May nineteen forty, that Prime Minister Paul Reynaud gave one of the most important speeches of the entire war — the speech that told the French people the situation was catastrophic, and that we were losing the war.

We'll come back to that in a few minutes. First, let's set the scene.

At the previous stop, we left off on the third of September nineteen thirty-nine, the day France and the United Kingdom declared war on Germany. Everything changed in Paris that day. The theatres, the cinemas and the music halls closed. Sandbags appeared around the monuments. Gas masks were handed out.

But at the front, almost nothing happened. Poland is crushed in barely two months, with Soviet help. But on the western front, at the French border, the Germans don't attack. Life in Paris slowly went back to normal — cinemas and theatres reopened, the restaurants filled up again. The French called this period the "phoney war". It lasted eight months.

But during that phoney war, the new Prime Minister Paul Reynaud, who takes office in March nineteen forty, knows the war is unavoidable — and he starts preparing Parisians for it.

From the first of April nineteen forty, food restrictions begin. Speciality breads are banned. Butter disappears from restaurant tables. Bakeries and butchers open on alternating days of the week. Nothing dramatic yet — but a clear signal that the war is closing in on the capital.

And on the tenth of May nineteen forty, the German attack on France begins. That's the start of that terrible thirty-five-day countdown between the opening of the German attack on France and, thirty-five days later, the fall of Paris into German hands.

The German offensive comes in two phases. And it's those two phases that explain how they reached Paris so easily. Phase one: the Germans attack in Belgium and the Netherlands — exactly as the French commander-in-chief, General Maurice Gamelin, had predicted. He then moves a million men north to stop them. Phase two, meanwhile, starts three days later, on the thirteenth of May. The Germans attack further south — through the Ardennes forest, which the French generals had declared impassable. But with a combined force of tanks and aircraft, the Germans cross the Ardennes and the river Meuse in barely two days, and break through the forest, the river, and the French line of defence.

And all at once, the road to Paris is open.

On the fifteenth of May at six in the morning, the Minister of Defence calls Paul Reynaud, the Prime Minister — he's the only person in government who really understands what has just happened. His words are these: "All is lost. The road to Paris is open. There is nothing left to stop the Germans reaching the capital."

Fortunately, the Minister of Defence had spoken a little too soon. On the night of the sixteenth of May, the news comes through: the Germans are not heading for Paris. They're racing first for the coast, to trap the Allied armies in the north.

One crucial thing to keep in mind: while all these events were unfolding inside the government, Parisians had absolutely no idea how serious the situation was. Censorship saw to that. The newspaper front pages of the sixteenth of May told Parisians that the Germans had been stopped, and that powerful counter-offensives were under way. None of that was true.

Now, back to the palace in front of you. On the twenty-first of May nineteen forty, Paul Reynaud came here, to this very place, to address the Senate. It's the speech where, for the first time, the political class heard the truth. He didn't hide it. He spoke openly of a rout of the French army. He told the senators that the cities of Arras and Amiens, major French cities a hundred and fifty kilometres north of Paris, were already in German hands.

Meanwhile, cooperation between the French and British armies begins to crack. The British realise that France might fall, and that they need to bring their two hundred and fifty thousand men home to defend their own island. The result is Dunkirk: between the twenty-sixth of May and the third of June, the entire British Expeditionary Force, along with some of the French soldiers, is evacuated across the Channel.

Then, in early June, Paris is bombed. On the third of June nineteen forty to be precise, German bombers hit factories in the fifteenth arrondissement. More than two hundred and fifty people are killed.

And on the tenth of June, four days before the Germans take Paris, Italy declares war on France. The German armies are now at the gates of the city. By the evening of the tenth of June, not a single minister is still in Paris. And neither are the Parisians. No clear instruction had been given to the population — but the exodus was massive. Out of two million eight hundred thousand Parisians, only around seven hundred thousand stayed. Nearly seventy-five per cent of the population had fled before the fourteenth of June.

Paris is then declared an open city on the twelfth of June. The Germans enter the capital in the early hours of the fourteenth of June. The Nazi flag is raised everywhere — on the Élysée, on the Arc de Triomphe, on the Eiffel Tower, and in many other places. On the morning of the fifteenth of June, every German unit parades down the Champs-Élysées to show the whole world that Paris has fallen.

A week later, Hitler makes his one and only visit to Paris. It's a rather strange visit, and a short one above all. He arrives around six in the morning on the twenty-third of June, tours the city — the Opéra, the Trocadéro, Napoleon's tomb at Les Invalides — and leaves again at nine. Three hours only in the city he had dreamed of conquering all his life. He would never return before his death in nineteen forty-five.
