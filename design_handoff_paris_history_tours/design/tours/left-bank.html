A Journey Through Wartime Paris
" Paris, Paris outraged! Paris broken! Paris martyred! But Paris liberated! — Charles de Gaulle, 25 August 1944

This Left Bank Tour explores WWII Paris through stories of the Fall, the Resistance, and the Liberation — guided through four historic stops in the streets where it happened. Each stop is charged with one of these three stories; each story is told at full length, with the archives and the voices behind it.

Did You Know?

Five things about wartime Paris

01

Paris was declared an "open city" on June 13, 1940 — meaning it would not be defended against the approaching German army, in order to spare its buildings and civilians from destruction.

02

During the four-year Nazi occupation, the Palais du Luxembourg served as the headquarters of the Luftwaffe — the German Air Force — in France.

03

Agnès Humbert, featured on our tour, was one of the founders of the Musée de l'Homme resistance network — one of the very first organised resistance groups in occupied Paris, formed in late 1940.

04

General Dietrich von Choltitz, the German military governor of Paris in August 1944, disobeyed Hitler's direct order to destroy the city's bridges and monuments before the Allied advance.

05

On August 26, 1944, General de Gaulle led a victory march from the Arc de Triomphe down the Champs-Élysées to Notre-Dame, despite active sniper fire along the route.

What You'll Discover

Three historical themes on WW2

Every stop belongs to one of three acts. Together they tell the story of Paris from the Fall of June 1940 to the Liberation of August 1944.

Act I · June 1940

The Fall of Paris

How Paris fell to the German army in only 35 days of active combat — the crucial decisions of that summer, and how a city decided it would rather survive than resist.

Act II · 1940 — 1944

The Resistance

How French Resistance fighters operated under the noses of the Nazi occupiers — ingenuity and courage, clandestine papers and networks, and the price they paid to protect their city and its culture.

Act III · August 1944

The Liberation

The Liberation of Paris in August 1944, when the city stood on the brink of destruction — and the famous characters, from De Gaulle to Hemingway, who lived through the week Paris came back to life.

The Itinerary

Our four historic stops

Walked in sequence — each stop sets up the next.

01
Introduction

Boulevard Saint-Michel

We begin on this iconic boulevard with a presentation of the tour and a quick history quiz to put everyone on the same page. You'll see how Paris looked on the eve of the German invasion — and the first signs of the war that still remain in the city's walls to this day.

  • Pre-war Paris — the city on the eve of invasion
  • Bullet holes and scars still visible today
  • The student quarter and its early Resistance cells
02
The Fall

Palais du Luxembourg

Walk through the gardens of this magnificent palace and discover how Paris fell to the German army in only 35 days of combat. We'll tell the story of the crucial weeks that changed the city forever — and how the Nazis transformed this symbol of French culture into their Air Force command centre for the duration of the occupation.

  • The 35-day campaign that ended the Battle of France
  • The Luftwaffe headquarters hidden in plain sight
  • Occupation-era Paris seen from its most surveilled garden
03
The Resistance

La Sorbonne

Near this historic university we tell the story of Agnès Humbert — a brave French Resistance fighter who co-founded the Musée de l'Homme network, one of the very first organised resistance groups in occupied Paris. You'll learn how these courageous individuals operated, what it cost them, and what they managed to achieve before the Gestapo closed in.

  • The Musée de l'Homme network — the first resistance cell
  • Clandestine papers and coded communications
  • The student insurrection of August 1944
04
The Liberation

Notre-Dame de Paris

We conclude at this iconic cathedral — witness to the Liberation of Paris in August 1944. Stand where General de Gaulle proclaimed the freedom of the capital and hear how close Paris came to being greatly damaged. The fighting on this square, and the Te Deum held under sniper fire in the galleries above, make for the most dramatic close of any WW2 walk in Paris.

  • De Gaulle's Te Deum under active sniper fire
  • The barricades of the Île de la Cité
  • Von Choltitz's refusal to destroy the city
