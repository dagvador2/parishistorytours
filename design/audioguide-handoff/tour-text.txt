S T O P 1
Context of War
60 Boulevard Saint-Michel — How we ended up at war in 1939
WHERE YOU STAND	📍
You are standing in front of 60 Boulevard Saint-Michel, in the 5th arrondissement,
just south of the Sorbonne and a short walk from the Panthéon.
Look up at the façade. You will see bullet holes peppering the stone — all the way
up to the top floor. The building was requisitioned by the German army during the
Occupation, and these scars are from the fighting during the Liberation of Paris in
August 1944. They are real, untouched, and a fitting place to begin.
A few hundred metres behind you sits the Panthéon, where Jean Moulin — the most
important figure of the French Resistance — lies entombed. It felt right to start the
tour here, between bullet holes and Jean Moulin.
Welcome
Thank you for being here. If you're reading this guide, it probably means you're
curious about both Paris and the Second World War — and this tour is designed to
bring the two together.
We'll do 4 main stops along 2 km, with a few short interstops along the way. This first
stop — this one — is a "catch-up" on how we ended up at war in 1939. The next will
cover the fall of Paris. Stop 3 will tell the story of the first Paris resistance network
through the eyes of Agnès Humbert. And we'll finish at Notre-Dame, with the
Liberation.
Let's go back to basics.
From Versailles to Munich
The First World War ends in 1918. The Germans sign the armistice inside a railway
carriage in the forest of Compiègne. The following year, they are forced to sign the
Treaty of Versailles — a treaty that brings Germany to its knees.

-- 4 of 38 --

The carriage in the Compiègne forest where the 1918 armistice was signed. Hitler would force the
French to sign the 1940 armistice in the same carriage.
❓ Do you know the three main types of sanction the treaty imposed on
Germany?
→ Territorial: Germany lost 15% of its land and all its colonies. Financial: 132
billion gold marks in reparations — equivalent to 47 kilotons of gold, or three
times the current annual budget of the French state. Military: no more armoured
vehicles, no air force, and the army capped at 100,000 men.
These sanctions stirred a powerful desire for revenge in Germany. The 1929 crash
drove poverty and unemployment through the roof, fascism spread, and Hitler seized
power in January 1933 — exactly ten years after his failed putsch attempt. Once in
power, he started tearing up the Versailles Treaty piece by piece: refusing to pay
what was owed, and above all rebuilding the German army.
France and the UK responded with pacifism. The central figure here is the British
Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain. He wanted to preserve peace at almost any
cost. Every time Hitler broke a clause of the treaty, the French and British said:
"We're not going to war over this. Peace matters more."

-- 5 of 38 --

Neville Chamberlain — British Prime Minister and the face of pre-war appeasement.
This way of thinking peaked at the Munich Agreement.
❓ Do you know what the Munich Agreement was, and when it was
signed?
→ In September 1938, Hitler and Chamberlain (along with France and Italy)
shook hands over a region of Czechoslovakia called the Sudetenland — a part of
the country with a large German-speaking population. Hitler was given the green
light to take it. Chamberlain came home waving the agreement, proud of his
"peace for our time." Six months later, Hitler broke the treaty and invaded the
rest of Czechoslovakia by force.

-- 6 of 38 --

Hitler and Chamberlain at Munich, September 1938.
"I have here a paper..." Chamberlain returns from Munich, the agreement in hand.
Finally, the French and the British changed their minds. They decided Poland was
the red line Hitler could not cross. And when Hitler invaded Poland anyway on 1
September 1939, France and the UK declared war two days later, on 3 September.

S T O P 2
The Fall of Paris
Palais du Luxembourg — How Paris fell in 35 days
WHERE YOU STAND	📍
You are standing in front of the Palais du Luxembourg, home of the French Senate
since 1799 — and in 1940, the seat of the upper house of Parliament.
It was here, on 21 May 1940, that Prime Minister Paul Reynaud gave one of the
most important speeches of the entire war — the speech that told the French
people that the situation was catastrophic.
We'll come back to that speech in a few minutes. First, let's set the scene.
The drôle de guerre — Paris during the "phoney war"
At the last stop we left things on 3 September 1939, the day France and the UK
declared war on Germany. Everything changed in Paris that day — theatres,
cinemas, and music halls all closed immediately. Sandbags went up around
monuments. Gas masks were distributed.
But on the front, almost nothing happened. Poland was crushed in a month, with
Soviet help. But on the Western front, the Germans did not attack. Life in Paris
slowly returned to normal — cinemas and theatres reopened, restaurants filled
again. The French called this period the drôle de guerre, the "phoney war." It lasted
eight months.
Paul Reynaud and the first restrictions
On 26 February 1940, the first German reconnaissance plane flew directly over
Paris. That was a turning point. The new Prime Minister Paul Reynaud, who had
taken office a month earlier, knew that war was inevitable — and he started
preparing Parisians for it.

-- 9 of 38 --

Paul Reynaud, Prime Minister of France from March 1940. He saw the war coming clearly — earlier
than most.
❓ From 1 April 1940, what changed for the people of Paris?
→ Food restrictions began. Special breads (raisin bread, milk bread) were
banned. Butter disappeared from restaurant tables. Bakeries and butcher shops
opened on alternating days of the week. None of this was dramatic yet — but it
was a clear signal that the easy months were over.
And on 10 May 1940, the easy months ended for good.
10 May 1940 — The German offensive
Look at the map. Two phases to understand:

The German offensive of May 1940. The dummy attack through Belgium and the Netherlands drew
the French army north — while the real attack came lower, through the Ardennes.
Phase 1: The Germans attack in Belgium and the Netherlands — exactly as the
French commander-in-chief, General Maurice Gamelin, had predicted. He moves
one million men north to meet them.
General Maurice Gamelin — commander-in-chief of the French army in May 1940.
Phase 2: On 13 May, the Germans attack lower — through the Ardennes forest,
which the French generals had declared "impassable" by armoured columns. Using a
combined force of tanks and Stuka dive-bombers, they cross the Ardennes and the
Meuse river in two days, slicing through the thin French defensive line in that region.
And just like that, the road to Paris is open.

-- 11 of 38 --

15–16 May 1940 — The Great Fear
On 15 May at 6 a.m., the Minister of Defence calls Paul Reynaud — he is the only
person in the government who really understands what has just happened. His
words: "Everything is lost. The road to Paris is open. There is nothing left to stop the
Germans from reaching the capital."
Reynaud spends 15 May assessing the damage. On 16 May at 9 a.m., he convenes an
emergency war committee at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs on the Quai d'Orsay, just
across the river from where we are now.
❓ What three urgent questions do you think dominated that meeting?
→ One — evacuate the government? Two — evacuate the population? Three —
what to do with all the classified documents in Paris, especially in the ministries?
The meeting was chaotic. The Ministry of Transport admitted it had neither the
trucks nor the trains to move the government within two days. So it was decided
that the government would stay until the very last moment, and the few trucks
available would be used to move the archives.
But as the meeting was coming to a close, people in the room began to hear muffled
noises from the courtyard outside. The employees of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs
were dumping every classified file out of the windows and into the courtyard. Once
the pile was high enough, they poured petrol on it and set it on fire. The order to burn
the files had come from the Secretary General of the Ministry, Alexis Léger, who
knew there was no way to evacuate 100 tonnes of archives in time.
And then, on the night of 16 May, the news came in: the Germans were not heading
for Paris. They were heading for the coast, to trap the Allied armies in the north.
The press kept the public in the dark
A crucial point: while all this was unfolding inside the government, the people of
Paris had absolutely no idea how bad things were. Censorship saw to that. The
newspaper front page from 16 May — the day the government was burning its own
archives — told Parisians the Germans had been "halted" and that "strong counter-
attacks" were underway. None of it was true.

-- 12 of 38 --

Le Petit Parisien, 16 May 1940. The reassuring tone bears no relation to the catastrophe unfolding on
the front.
21 May 1940 — Reynaud's speech at the Luxembourg
Look back at the palace. On 21 May 1940 — five days after the great fear — Paul
Reynaud came here to address the Senate. This was the speech where, for the first
time, the political class was told the truth. He didn't hide it. He spoke of a "rout" of
the French army, of a "fault, perhaps a crime" in the conduct of operations. He
announced that General Maxime Weygand would replace Gamelin as commander-in-
chief, and that Marshal Philippe Pétain — the hero of Verdun — would join the
government as deputy Prime Minister. Two years later, Pétain would be the man
leading Vichy and the collaboration with the Germans.
From Dunkirk to the Fall
Meanwhile, the cooperation between the French and the British army began to fall
apart. The British were realising that France might fall — and that they needed to
bring their 250,000 men home to defend their own island. The result was Dunkirk:
between 26 May and 3 June, the entire British Expeditionary Force, along with a
portion of French soldiers, was evacuated across the Channel.

-- 13 of 38 --

3 June — Paris is bombed
Many people think Paris was never bombed in the Second World War, because it was
declared an open city. That's true — but only from 12 June. On 3 June 1940, German
bombers struck factories in the 15th arrondissement. More than 250 people died.
The aftermath of the bombing of Paris, 3 June 1940.
On 10 June, Italy declared war on France. The German armies were now at the gates
of the city. By the evening of the 10th, every minister had fled Paris.
And so had the Parisians. No clear instructions had been given to the population —
but the exodus was massive. Of the 2.8 million Parisians, only around 700,000
stayed. About 75% of the population had fled by 14 June.
14 June 1940 — The Germans enter Paris
Paris was declared an open city on 12 June. The Germans entered the capital early in
the morning of 14 June. The Nazi flag went up everywhere — on the Élysée Palace, all
along Rue de Rivoli, on the Arc de Triomphe, on the Eiffel Tower.

-- 14 of 38 --

The Nazi flag flying over the Arc de Triomphe, June 1940.
German military headquarters were set up in the Crillon hotel, on Place de la
Concorde. On the morning of 15 June, every German unit paraded down the Champs-
Élysées to show the world that Paris had fallen.
A week later, Hitler made his only ever visit to Paris. He arrived around 6 a.m. on 23
June, toured the city — the Opéra, the Trocadéro, Napoleon's tomb at the Invalides —
and was gone by 9 a.m. Three hours in the city he had dreamed of conquering his
whole adult life.

-- 15 of 38 --

Hitler at the Trocadéro, 23 June 1940 — the most reproduced image of his only visit to Paris.
Hitler at the Trocadéro with the Eiffel Tower behind him.

-- 16 of 38 --

And finally, on 22 June 1940, France signed the armistice. Hitler had it signed in the
very same railway carriage in the Compiègne forest where the Germans had signed
in 1918. The carriage was then taken to Berlin as a trophy.
France divided after the armistice: occupied zone (north and west), "free" zone under Vichy (south),
and the annexed Alsace-Moselle.

It is a fitting introduction to what is coming next. The FFI didn't fight a conventional

possible — and crucially, to gather intelligence and pass it to the Allies, so that the
French and American armies could enter Paris with some idea of what they were
walking into.
Jacques Guierre was barely 20 years old. He died on this square, on this very
morning. The plaque is the only mark of it.

S T O P 3
The Beginnings of the Resistance
Rue Monsieur-le-Prince / Vaugirard — Agnès Humbert and the Musée de l'Homme
network
WHERE YOU STAND	📍
You are at the corner of Rue Monsieur-le-Prince and Rue de Vaugirard, in the 6th
arrondissement. Nothing here marks the spot — and that is partly the point.
In the autumn of 1940, a small group of Paris intellectuals began meeting in this
neighbourhood to plan what would become France's first organised resistance
network. These streets, this quarter — they were the kind of unremarkable Left
Bank backstreets where a resistance cell could meet without drawing attention.
We're going to tell you the story of one of those meetings, and of the woman at its
centre.
Agnès Humbert
Agnès Humbert (1894–1963) — art historian, museum curator, and one of the founders of the first
organised resistance network in Paris.
At the previous stop we left things on 14 June 1940 — the day Paris fell. Eight days
later, on 22 June, France signed the armistice. After that, slowly, the Parisians who
had fled south started coming home. So did the staff of the great Paris museums,
including Agnès Humbert, an art historian working at the Musée National des Arts et
Traditions Populaires.

-- 19 of 38 --

She returned at the beginning of August 1940. Very quickly, she noticed what had
changed.
❓ What do you think were the main changes inside the French
museums under occupation?
→ Everything was now labelled in German on top of French. Museums were free
of charge for German soldiers. And entire exhibitions had been altered or
removed — photographs of the USSR that Agnès Humbert had taken were gone,
as were photos of the 1936 French general strikes. The Occupation reached into
every institution.
Why the museums mattered
Before we get to the resistance network itself, a word about why museums sit at the
heart of this story. Every great Paris museum was caught up in the war. The Jeu de
Paume, just over the river, became the central depot for art looted from Jewish
collections across Europe. Hermann Göring — the second man of the Reich — visited
the Jeu de Paume at least twenty times, hand-picking the pieces he wanted shipped
to Germany.
The Musée de l'Homme at the Trocadéro — the museum that gave its name to the network.
Across town, at the Trocadéro, the Musée de l'Homme — an anthropology museum
opened just three years earlier — became something stranger: a meeting point for
intellectuals who refused to accept the Occupation. The network we're about to talk
about was named after it.

-- 20 of 38 --

"It's as if we were rats in a trap"
Agnès Humbert wrote those words in her diary that summer. She had decided she
could not live with the Occupation. The question was — what to do about it?
When you decide to resist, the first thing you do is look for allies. That's exactly what
she did. Her closest ally was Jean Cassou, a friend and fellow museum curator.
Together they began meeting trusted acquaintances, one by one.
Jean Cassou — writer, art historian, co-founder of the Musée de l'Homme network.
By early September 1940, they had gathered a small group of around ten people they
trusted absolutely. They met every week, often at 14 Rue de l'Abbaye in the 6th
arrondissement — a few streets from where you're standing. At their meeting place,
even the small detail of the clock mattered: it was kept on French time, not on the
German time the Occupation had imposed across France. A tiny, daily act of
defiance.
From scraps of paper to a clandestine newspaper
Their goal was to inform. They wanted to publish a clandestine newspaper — to tell
Parisians the truths the censored press would not. They started small: hand-written
notes on scraps of paper, left on metro benches, in mailboxes, on café tables. By the
end of September they had a roneo, an early stencil duplicator, and could produce
dozens of copies at once.

-- 21 of 38 --

On 15 December 1940, the group published the first edition of their clandestine
newspaper. They called it Résistance — and the word would stick. After the war, this
is what we would call the whole movement.
The first issue of Résistance, December 1940. The newspaper that gave the entire French resistance
its name.
They wrote about the military situation as they understood it; messages of hope and
motivation; and on the practical side, detailed accounts of how French food was
being shipped to Germany — precise counts of cows, pigs, chickens, and wine taken
from French farms. The point was to make Parisians see what occupation actually
was, beneath the polite surface.

-- 22 of 38 --

Notre Guerre — Agnès Humbert's diary, published in 1946.
How many editions before they were caught?
The newspaper was meant to come out every two weeks. They managed just four
editions before the network was dismantled. By early 1941 the Gestapo had arrived
in Paris and was focusing on networks like this one. The problem with the Musée de
l'Homme network was that it was growing fast — absorbing smaller networks,
diversifying its work. At one point Agnès Humbert hid a Polish pilot. The network
was also brought plans of a German submarine base on the Atlantic coast by a
French officer.
And eventually — as is almost always the case with networks that grow too quickly —
they brought in someone they should not have trusted. His name was Albert Gaveau.
He worked for the Gestapo. One by one, people began to be arrested. Agnès
Humbert was arrested on 13 April 1941.
The Germans came for her at the hospital where she was visiting her mother. They
drove her home and searched her flat. She was interrogated at the Gestapo
headquarters on Rue des Saussaies, then moved through a series of Paris prisons.
The network was destroyed. She survived the camps, returned to France after the
war, published her diary as Notre Guerre in 1946, and lived quietly until her death in
1963.

More than 600 barricades were built across Paris during the insurrection week. They
were made of cobblestones, café tables, café chairs, café signs, mattresses, doors —
anything heavy enough to slow a German tank. The Germans had about 22,000 men
and 100 tanks in Paris that week. The barricades were a logistical nightmare for
them: every time they tried to move armour across the city, they had to send infantry
first to dismantle blockages, often under fire from the windows above.

S T O P 4
The Liberation of Paris
Facing Notre-Dame — The Préfecture de Police and the insurrection of August
1944
WHERE YOU STAND	📍
You are facing Notre-Dame, with the Île de la Cité in front of you. But the building
this stop is really about is the one immediately to the right of Notre-Dame, on the
same Île de la Cité: the Préfecture de Police de Paris.
The Préfecture was the first stronghold seized by the resistance during the
insurrection of August 1944. It is where the Liberation of Paris really began.
Paris could have been Warsaw
Before we get into what happened here, you need to understand the broader picture.
Paris in August 1944 came terrifyingly close to suffering the same fate as Warsaw.
Warsaw's insurrection began on 1 August 1944, just three weeks before the Paris
uprising. It lasted more than 60 days. By the end, 18,000 Polish soldiers and 170,000
civilians were dead. 85% of the city was destroyed. The Soviets, who were within
striking distance, did not come to the Poles' aid.
Now look at Paris in August 1944:
● A city occupied by the Nazis
● An insurrection started by its own people
● Allies who were not originally planning to liberate the city
● A new German commander appointed by a dictator with nothing left to lose
Sounds familiar?
❓ Why didn't the Allies plan to liberate Paris straight away?
→ Three reasons. One — the fear of street-by-street fighting like Stalingrad,
costly in both soldiers and civilians. Two — Paris was not strategically essential.
Going through it would delay the American advance on Berlin, where the race
against the Soviets had become the priority. Three — the logistical nightmare of
suddenly having to feed 3 million starving Parisians once the city was liberated.
And yet, the Allies came
The Allies had landed in Normandy on 6 June 1944, and the original plan was to
sweep east straight for Berlin, bypassing Paris. What changed their minds was the
insurrection itself, and what Raoul Nordling — the Swedish consul, more on him in a

-- 27 of 38 --

minute — was able to tell Eisenhower about Von Choltitz. By 22 August, the decision
had been taken: Leclerc and his men would rush in. The French 2nd Armoured
Division and the US 4th Infantry Division would come to Paris' aid.
The main characters
Let me introduce you to the protagonists of that terrible week in Paris.
On the German side: General Dietrich von Choltitz, brought to Paris by Hitler himself
on 10 August. His mission: keep Paris German, and crush any insurrection without
mercy.
General Dietrich von Choltitz — German commander of "Greater Paris", August 1944.
On the French side, two camps were already competing for political power after
Paris would be liberated:
● The Communists, who led most of the resistance fighters inside Paris.
● The Gaullists, loyal to General Charles de Gaulle. They controlled most of
the police force inside Paris, and crucially they had General Philippe Leclerc
— who was leading De Gaulle's French 2nd Armoured Division, cruising
eastward alongside the Allies after the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944.

19 August 1944 — The insurrection begins
By mid-August, Paris was boiling. The police, the gas workers, the electricity
workers, the transport workers were all on strike. The Germans were openly
preparing to defend the city. Von Choltitz had told the mayor of Paris that he was
ready to destroy every bridge in the city and every power station.
The Gaullists wanted to delay the insurrection — it would be a bloodbath for
Parisians. The Communists wanted to launch it immediately, to take political control
of the city by force of arms. The Communists got their way. The insurrection began
on 19 August 1944 — the 1,518th day of the German occupation.
The call to insurrection plastered on Paris walls, August 1944.
The Préfecture de Police — first stronghold
Even though the Communists had wanted to fire the first shot, the Gaullists heard
about it and moved fast. It would have been too dangerous to let the Communists
alone claim the symbol of the insurrection. So a senior policeman, Yves Bayet, led the
Paris police force in seizing the Préfecture de Police for General de Gaulle. He then
walked to the terrace of the café Les Deux Magots and told Charles Luizet — De
Gaulle's trusted lieutenant, parachuted into France just seven days earlier — that the
Préfecture was his.
First gunfire in Paris since 1871
It was the first time the sound of gunfire and gunpowder had echoed through Paris
since the Commune of 1871. At 3:30 p.m., the Germans sent the tanks. Two Panthers
and a captured Renault rolled onto the parvis of Notre-Dame — directly in front of
you, where the tourists now stand — and opened fire. The main door of the
Préfecture exploded.

Von Choltitz, Nordling, and the truce
Faced with this open revolt, Von Choltitz had to act fast. His plan: a Luftwaffe air
strike on the Préfecture at 4 a.m. on 20 August, followed by a ground assault by the
tanks. The Préfecture would be flattened.
It didn't happen. The Swedish consul-general in Paris, Raoul Nordling, intervened
and proposed a ceasefire that suited both sides — Von Choltitz needed time to
consolidate, the resistance needed to resupply. The truce was agreed.
It did not hold. The Communists pushed hard against it — they wanted the
insurrection to keep its momentum. Rol-Tanguy, that day, said the line that would
become famous: "Paris is worth 200,000 dead."
All over Paris, the city organised for war: clandestine printing presses now produced
flyers with Molotov cocktail recipes; pharmacies, which stocked potassium chlorate,
became improvised arsenals; the flats of resistance medical students became field
hospitals.
The clandestine press of those days — calling Parisians "To the barricades!"

22–23 August — The decision to send Leclerc
On the night of 22 August, after Nordling crossed the Allied lines and pleaded with
Eisenhower and Bradley, the decision was finally taken: the French 2nd Armoured
Division under General Leclerc, supported by the US 4th Infantry, would rush to
Paris. Leclerc had been ordered to wait. He started moving anyway.
On 23 August, Von Choltitz received a direct order from Hitler himself. If the Allies
were to reach Paris, they should find only ruins. Across the city, the explosives were
already in place: the 42 bridges, the Parliament, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the
Luxembourg Palace, the two hotels on Place de la Concorde, the Invalides.
Everything was wired. Choltitz was waiting on the final order.
24 August — The plane mission to the Préfecture
By the morning of 24 August, the situation for the resistance fighters inside the
Préfecture was desperate. They were almost out of ammunition. Luizet and his
deputy Pisani called the police station in Longjumeau, 35 km south of Paris, to ask
where the Allied divisions were. The man on the other end held the phone to the
window and said: "Can you hear that? They're right here."
And then there is a story I really want to tell you.
On the afternoon of 24 August, hours before the Allied tanks would enter the city,
General Leclerc ordered two French pilots to carry out an extraordinary mission.
Their job: fly over Paris and drop a written message into the courtyard of the
Préfecture de Police, to tell the resistance fighters inside: hold on, we are coming.

Captain Jean Callet was the pilot. Lieutenant Étienne Mantoux was in the back,
holding the message. The mission launched around 5 p.m. They flew low, hiding in
the clouds where they could. By 6 p.m. they were over Paris and could see the
objective ahead — but the Germans could see them too.
Eight hundred metres from the Préfecture, as Callet was following the Seine, he
abruptly nosed the plane into a dive. He wanted the Germans to think he'd been hit
and was crashing, to make them stop firing. At the last possible moment, he pulled
up — and Mantoux, in the back, dropped the message. It landed inside the Préfecture
courtyard.
The flight path of the mission — over Paris, towards the Préfecture, and back.

-- 35 of 38 --

The official report of the mission, filed after the pilots' return.
Getting away, they flew as close to the rooftops as they dared, zigzagging hard. The
landing gear of the Piper was shot up over southern Paris. The plane had 13 bullet
holes when they finally landed in a field in Allied-held territory, tipped over on its
right wing.
Jean Callet survived the war and became a general in the French army. He died in
2006. Étienne Mantoux was not so lucky — he was killed on 29 April 1945, less than
ten days before Victory in Europe.
24 August at night — The bells of Paris
At 9:22 p.m. on 24 August, the first detachment of the French 2nd Armoured
Division, led by Captain Raymond Dronne, reached the Hôtel de Ville. The resistance
fighters inside burst into La Marseillaise. The song spread to the streets. On the
radio, an announcer asked all the priests of Paris to ring their bells — for the first
time in four years, since they had stayed silent throughout the Occupation. The great
13-tonne bell of Notre-Dame, the one named Emmanuel, the one directly in front of
you now, rang out around 9:30 p.m.

25 August — Liberation
On the morning of 25 August, the French 2nd Armoured Division and the US 4th
Infantry made their proper entrance into Paris. The streets erupted. Parisians threw
everything they had — fruit, cheese, bottles of wine and champagne saved for exactly
this moment — at the soldiers. Some tanks were reportedly slowed down by the
sheer weight of civilians climbing onto them.
